## Proposed addition

The instruction “יבדק,” “let it be investigated,” makes the archon’s accession a fact that must be established before the document can be dated. Knowing the title’s meaning is insufficient. The preceding anecdote about Rabbi supplies a useful comparison: a tied document appears postdated until Zunin explains a convention for counting regnal years attributed to “this nation.” “Such is this nation’s custom” appears in both discussions. Read together, the explanations illustrate a practical difficulty of documentary interpretation: a date can be intelligible within the issuing community’s conventions while misleading an outside reader. The text even records that Rabbi learned the earlier convention from Zunin. This comparison does not establish that the two discussions were composed as a deliberate pair.

The two objections test different ways in which the title could fail to specify a year. The first proposes that the wording refers to an extended reign; the reply assigns different designations to the first and second years. The second asks whether the ruler was removed and reinstated. This possibility would make an accession date ambiguous even after the annual terminology was understood. The reply supplies the compound designation “archon digon.” On this reading, the title distinguishes continued service from renewed appointment, although “they called that one” could report a particular case rather than formulate a universal rule. Greek *archōn* could designate the eponymous magistrate after whom a year was named. The analogy is limited: annual magistracy differs from the extended reign imagined here, and Greek usage does not independently authenticate the archon/digon convention. The identification as Rome in the post is an editorial gloss.

The recurrence of *digon* connects the dating discussion to the two legal lists that follow. Its function changes with the setting. In the nazirite formula, the expressions determine how many terms of naziriteship the speaker undertakes. In the house list, they describe configurations of walls. For the first item in the vow list, *heina*, Sumakhos rules one term; describing it as a “henagon” imports a geometric interpretation that the text does not give. The sequence therefore shows more than the presence of Greek vocabulary in rabbinic discourse. A borrowed expression acquires a particular legal consequence through the kind of statement in which it appears. The lexical connection is observable; a deliberate, comprehensive theory of language behind the sequence remains an interpretation.

The explanation immediately after the excerpt clarifies the house classification. Bava Batra 164b.12 counts the repeated plural “walls” in Leviticus 14 as two plus two, yielding four. The preceding ruling includes a four-sided house while excluding both a triangle and a pentagon. Read together, the ruling and its explanation treat four as an exact requirement for the configurations listed, rather than a minimum that also admits the pentagon. Greek terminology supplies a way to describe the alternatives; scriptural interpretation determines which alternative falls under the law. This distinction also cautions against deriving the impurity rule from geometry itself or assuming that the three discussions use their shared vocabulary in precisely the same way.
