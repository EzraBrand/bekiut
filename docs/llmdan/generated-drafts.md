## Counting by Title and by Corner: A Reading of Bava Batra 164b

*AI-generated interpretation, unreviewed by the author.*

The archon passage ([164b.7–9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.164b.7)) sits between two anecdotes about Rabbi and tied documents ([164b.5–6](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.164b.5) and [164b.13](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.164b.13)). In both, a date is misread or cannot be found. The formula "כך מנהגה של אומה זו" appears twice: once in Zunin's correction of Rabbi and once in Rav Hoshaya's answer. Both times it explains how a foreign regime counts regnal years. My interpretation is that the sugya treats foreign dating as practical knowledge obtained from informants. Rabbi himself is said to have learned it from Zunin. The text says only "this nation." Identifying it as Rome is an editorial gloss, and the sugya never names the nation.

Each of the two objections tests whether the title alone can fix a date. The first ([164b.8](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.164b.8)) reads ארכן as Aramaic, "his reign extended." On that reading, counting from the ruler's accession would be wrong. The answer is that custom reserves "archon" for the first year and calls the second year "digon." The second objection ([164b.9](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.164b.9)) raises deposition and reinstatement. The answer is that such a ruler is called "archon digon." Both answers add precision to the title, and neither adds anything to the document. The system works only if titles are exhaustive. The text is also silent on some points. It does not name the third year, and "trigon" would be a reader's inference. Rabbi Ḥanina's "יבדק" assumes that someone can look up when the archon took office.

Greek usage gives partial support. LSJ (ἄρχων II) records the eponymous archon, so dating "in the year of so-and-so, archon" has a real Greek analogue. That does not confirm the Bavli's archon/digon sequence, and nothing in this packet attests it elsewhere. The convention should be treated as the Talmud's report. Whether "digon" reflects an informant's rendering or a local formula is speculation.

The word digon links the dating passage to the two baraitot that follow, but the baraitot use the vocabulary differently. In the nazirite list ([164b.10](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.164b.10)), the terms are numerals that multiply the vow term. Hina means "one." It is not a "-gon," so the list does not assume a one-sided figure, and the overview's "henagon" goes beyond the text. The printed [Tosefta Nazir 1:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Tosefta_Nazir.1.2) has a shorter list with variant spellings. In the impurity list ([164b.11](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.164b.11)), the terms describe the shapes of houses. A round house is grouped with the excluded shapes, and so is the pentagon. LSJ's τρίγωνος confirms the triangular sense. The dating use is closer to the numeral use.

The derivation ([164b.12](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.164b.12)) reads "קירות" in [Leviticus 14:37](https://www.sefaria.org/Leviticus.14.37) and 14:39 as a minimal plural of two each, which totals four. This explains why digon and trigon houses are excluded. Excluding the pentagon requires reading four as an exact count, and the derivation does not say this explicitly. [Mishnah Negaim 12:1](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Negaim.12.1) excludes round and triangular houses and includes a four-sided one "even on four pillars." It does not mention a pentagon. The printed [Tosefta Negaim 6:3](https://www.sefaria.org/Tosefta_Negaim.6.3) has garbled-looking forms. As I read it, a count of walls works here much as a count of years works in the contract.

## "What Did You Do?": A Reading of Shabbat 156a–b

*AI-generated interpretation, unreviewed by the author.*

The stories follow a debate. Rabbi Ḥanina holds that "there is mazal for Israel," and Rabbi Yoḥanan denies it, citing Jeremiah 10:2 ([156a.12](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.156a.12)). Rav's proof tells Abraham to "go out from your astrology" ([156a.14](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.156a.14)). God then says he will move Jupiter from west to east ([156b.1](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.156b.1)). In this proof the planets still matter, and God rearranges them. Each story is then introduced with "ומד... נמי אין מזל לישראל." Ablet and the Chaldeans are right about the danger, since real snakes appear. What the sugya denies is that a forecast is inexorable for Israel. That position differs from a modern scientific rejection of astrology.

Both snakes are found dead after the fact. One is cut in two inside a burden. The other is pierced through the eye and drawn out on the pin the next morning. Neither person notices the danger. In both stories the sage asks "מאי עבדת?" The overview omits the answers, which carry the stories' explanatory weight. The worker in Shmuel's story saw that one man had no bread for the shared meal and pretended to take bread from him "so that he would not be embarrassed" ([156b.3](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.156b.3)). His motive is stated, and the act is staged so the gift stays hidden. Rabbi Akiva's daughter gave away "the portion that you gave me" to a poor man ([156b.5](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.156b.5)). Shmuel and Rabbi Akiva each go out and teach the same sermon, almost word for word, on "צדקה תציל ממות": the verse means death itself, and not only an unusual death.

Some details about the daughter need care. The Chaldeans told Rabbi Akiva (אמרי ליה), and "הוה דאיג" is masculine, so it most naturally means he was worried. The common translation "she was very worried" changes the subject. As a result, the text does not establish that she knew of the prediction. It also gives no motive for her act, so neither self-rescue nor pure altruism can be shown. What the text does stress is perception. Everyone was busy with the feast, "and nobody heard him." She heard. In [Proverbs 10:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Proverbs.10.2) and [11:4](https://www.sefaria.org/Proverbs.11.4), the same clause uses tzedakah to mean righteousness, and the sermons narrow it to almsgiving. Tobit 12:8–9, which says almsgiving saves from death, shows the association is older. That parallel does not suggest the Talmud depends on Tobit.

The third story ends differently ([156b.6](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.156b.6)). Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak does not know why his mother insists that he keep his head covered. The cloak falls, he looks up, the impulse overcomes him, and he bites off a cluster of dates. No one asks "what did you do," and no sermon follows. The text does not say whose tree it was. The phrase "that did not belong to him" is the translator's addition, though the theft is implied by the prediction. My interpretation is that the story works as a proof in reverse: the prediction was held off only while the practice continued. A further possibility, which is speculation, is a link to the first half of Proverbs 10:2, about ill-gotten treasure.

Ptolemy offers a partial comparison. In Tetrabiblos I.3 (Robbins, Loeb 1940, pp. 23–33), he argues that some predictions can be averted by countervailing causes. His causes are natural, and he admits that some events are unavoidable. In this sugya the countervailing causes are mitzvot, prayer, and fear of Heaven. The comparison shows a shared problem. It does not show that the rabbis borrowed from Ptolemy.
