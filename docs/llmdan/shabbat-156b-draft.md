## Proposed addition

The dead snakes give these stories a particular argumentative force. The predicted danger leaves physical evidence even though the predicted death does not occur. Ablat finds a snake cut in two; Rabbi Akiva’s daughter discovers that her pin has pierced a snake’s eye. The predictions are thus partly corroborated within the narratives themselves. Read against the preceding debate over whether Israel is subject to *mazal*, the stories distinguish a recognizable threat from an unavoidable outcome. This is consistent with the immediately preceding account of God changing Abraham’s astrological circumstances. It would be misleading to turn the sequence into a uniform rabbinic dismissal of astrology: the fuller discussion records disagreement, and these stories retain the danger the prediction identifies.

In the two snake stories, discovering the dead animal is followed by the same question: “מאי עבדת,” “What did you do?” The answers, omitted from the post’s abbreviated quotations, supply the explanation. Shmuel’s interlocutor concealed a companion’s inability to contribute bread to their shared meal. His stated purpose was “so that he would not be embarrassed.” In the daughter’s account, a poor man called at the door while the wedding guests were occupied with the feast; she rose and gave him her own portion. Both accounts focus on noticing another person’s vulnerability. The man protects someone from public exposure; the daughter responds to someone the surrounding company fails to hear. The retrospective inquiry makes these easily overlooked acts central to the explanation of survival.

Each sage then expounds the same scriptural clause, “וצדקה תציל ממות,” “righteousness delivers from death,” applying it to deliverance from death itself. The charitable act is therefore connected to the outcome by an explicit interpretation within the story. Tobit 12:8–9 provides an earlier Jewish comparison for the association between almsgiving and deliverance from death, without establishing direct literary dependence. The daughter’s motive, however, remains unspecified. The astrologers address Rabbi Akiva, and the masculine phrase “הוה דאיג,” “he was worried,” naturally continues with him as its subject. The translation’s attribution of worry to his daughter should not become evidence that she knew the prediction. Neither a calculated attempt to save herself nor a wholly disinterested motive can be established from the wording.

The final story changes the outcome. Rav Naḥman bar Yitzḥak’s mother refuses to let him uncover his head, telling him to fear Heaven and ask for mercy. He does not understand why she insists on this practice. When the covering falls, his desire overcomes him and he bites off a cluster from a palm tree. The opening prediction that he will become a thief supplies the context for this ending, although ownership of the tree is not specified in the Aramaic. There is no concluding rescue or repeated sermon. The episode suggests that restraint depended on the practice, but its evidentiary function under “there is no mazal for Israel” is left implicit. The English translation’s concluding explanation about overcoming an inborn inclination through education and prayer is a gloss. The narrative itself ends with the lapse, leaving readers to reconcile this outcome with the proposition the story was introduced to support.
