Using AI in Jewish textual research

AI systems can speed up finding and drafting. They cannot replace sources. Treat every output as a lead to be checked.

  1. Retrieve, then cite

    Prefer tools that retrieve from a named corpus and show the passage. Open every cited source yourself and confirm the reference (tractate, folio, chapter, edition) actually says what is claimed.

  2. Return to scans and editions

    Digital text is a derivative. For anything that matters, check the manuscript images, early printings, or critical edition — and record which edition you used.

  3. Expect hallucinations

    Language models invent plausible quotations, authors, page numbers, and bibliographic entries. A confident answer is not evidence. Absence of a source is a reason to stop.

  4. OCR and translation errors

    Hebrew and Aramaic OCR confuses similar letters and mangles abbreviations; machine translation flattens technical terms. Compare against the image and against a human translation where one exists.

  5. Privacy and copyright

    Do not paste unpublished work, private correspondence, or restricted materials into third-party services without permission. Respect licences of editions and translations.

  6. No automated halakhic authority

    AI output is not a ruling. Practical questions of Jewish law belong with a qualified, accountable authority who knows the person and the case.

  7. Tools versus scholarship about AI

    Distinguish AI tools (search, transcription, translation, chat interfaces) from scholarship about AI and Jewish texts. The directory labels these where possible; a listing is not an endorsement.
